XUANZANG'S ACCOUNT OF KAMARUPA

Source: Si-Yu-Ki: Buddhist Records of the Western World, translated by Samuel Beal, volume II, Book X, printed pp.195–199; supplied PDF pp.223–227. Read the Kamarupa section beginning near the foot of p.195 and ending near the top of p.199. The neighbouring descriptions of Pundravardhana and Samatata belong to other countries.

PERIOD AND CHARACTER OF THE SOURCE

This is a translated seventh-century travel account concerning the kingdom under Bhaskaravarman, whom the text also calls Kumara. It combines descriptions of the country, information obtained from others, a court encounter and religious judgements. This summary follows Beal's English translation and checks its page images. It is not a fresh translation of the Chinese.

LAND, SETTLEMENT AND ECONOMY

The account gives Kamarupa a circuit of about 10,000 li and its capital a circuit of about 30 li. It describes low, fertile, cultivated country, with panasa and narikela fruit trees. Beal identifies these in a footnote as breadfruit and coconut; panasa is often rendered as jackfruit. Water from rivers and embanked reservoirs circulated around towns. The climate is presented as mild. The account does not give a modern map, coordinates or a secure conversion of these measurements into present-day kilometres.

SOCIETY AND LANGUAGE

Xuanzang describes the people as having strong memories and an interest in study, and says that their language differed somewhat from that of central India. His comments about appearance, temperament and behaviour belong to a traveller's broad characterisation. They are not measurements of an entire population and should not be used as direct modern ethnic classifications.

RELIGION

He reports about 100 Deva temples and numerous followers of non-Buddhist religious traditions. He says that the country lacked Buddhist monasteries and that the Buddhists who were present practised privately. These are claims made in his account of the period and places known to him; they do not prove that Buddhism never existed anywhere within every territory later called Kamarupa. The ruler is presented as personally outside the Buddhist faith but respectful of learned Buddhist monks.

BHASKARAVARMAN AND ROYAL LEARNING

The king is named Bhaskaravarman and styled Kumara. The account associates him with Narayana ancestry and calls him a Brahmin. It depicts a ruler interested in learning who attracted talented visitors. Its claim of a thousand ancestral successions is a dynastic tradition, not a usable chronological list. Royal caste and ancestry claims should be compared with the Dubi, Nidhanpur and seal genealogies rather than accepted as independently established descent.

INVITATION AND COURT ENCOUNTER

Bhaskaravarman repeatedly invited the traveller from Nalanda. Silabhadra encouraged him to accept. In their reported conversation, the king praised the traveller's pursuit of learning, asked about China and expressed admiration for its ruler. This is evidence for the account's representation of diplomatic curiosity and the prestige of scholarly visitors. The dialogue is a transmitted literary account, not a verbatim court transcript.

RELATIONS WITH HARSHAVARDHANA

Bhaskaravarman says that Siladitya, the ruler identified with Harshavardhana, had summoned him to an assembly at Kajughira. He asks Xuanzang to accompany him, and the narrative says they went together. This is valuable for studying contact between the Kamarupa court and Harsha's political and religious gatherings. It does not, by itself, define their entire political relationship as either subordination or an equal alliance.

ROUTES AND FRONTIERS

The approach to Kamarupa is described as about 900 li eastward from the preceding country, crossing a great river. The main translated passage does not name that river here. To the east, it describes hills and proximity to peoples associated in the Chinese terminology with the southwestern frontier of China. A route of about two months towards the frontier of Sichuan is reported from inquiry, not narrated as a journey Xuanzang himself completed.

The southeast is described as a region of wild elephants used in warfare. The next itinerary gives Samatata about 1,200–1,300 li to the south. These are relative geographical observations. They do not establish that the kingdom occupied all the intervening land or corresponded to modern Assam's borders.

WHAT BELONGS TO BEAL'S FOOTNOTES

The note at the bottom of printed p.195 identifies the capital with Pragjyotisha and discusses the Karatoya and a kingdom encompassing several later districts. That is Beal's nineteenth-century editorial geography, citing other authorities. Those particular statements must not be quoted as the wording of Xuanzang's main narrative. Likewise, the twenty monasteries and 3,000 monks described above the Kamarupa heading belong to the preceding country, not Kamarupa.

HISTORICAL VALUE

The account is especially useful for seventh-century royal patronage, settlement, water management, religion, routes and relations with neighbouring powers. It should be used alongside contemporary inscriptions and archaeological evidence. For linguistic or boundary arguments, compare a newer translation and the Chinese text before drawing a precise conclusion.
